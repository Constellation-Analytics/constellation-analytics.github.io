How I built a churn prediction engine that saved $1.2M

A walkthrough of the end-to-end ML pipeline I built to identify at-risk customers 90 days before they churned — from messy raw data to a live model integrated into the CRM.

Background

The business was losing roughly 8% of its customer base per month and had no systematic way of knowing who was at risk until they'd already cancelled. The customer success team was working from gut feel, which meant they were spending time on the wrong accounts.

I was brought in to build a data-driven early warning system — something that could surface at-risk customers 90 days out so the team had time to actually intervene.

"We were essentially waiting for people to leave before we noticed them. We needed to flip that completely." — Head of Customer Success

Defining the problem

Before touching any data, I spent two weeks with the customer success and product teams to understand what "churn" actually meant in their context. There were three distinct churn types, and the team had historically lumped them all together.

  • Voluntary churn — customer actively cancels
  • Involuntary churn — payment failure leads to cancellation
  • Downsell churn — customer drops to a lower plan

The model needed to focus on voluntary churn only. Involuntary churn was a billing problem, not a product problem, and mixing the two would muddy the signal.

Data & feature engineering

I pulled 18 months of historical data from Snowflake across five source systems: product usage logs, support tickets, billing events, NPS survey responses, and CRM notes.

The richest predictive signal turned out to be feature inactivity sequences — not just whether a user had logged in, but which specific features they had stopped using in which order.
Python — feature engineering snippet
# Calculate rolling 30-day feature engagement score
df['feature_engagement_30d'] = (
    df.groupby('customer_id')['feature_events']
    .transform(lambda x: x.rolling(30).sum())
)

# Flag customers with declining engagement trend
df['engagement_slope'] = (
    df.groupby('customer_id')['feature_engagement_30d']
    .transform(lambda x: linregress(range(len(x)), x).slope)
)

Model selection & training

I tested four model architectures before landing on XGBoost as the final approach. Logistic regression gave decent recall but couldn't capture the non-linear interaction between feature usage and support ticket volume.

Model AUC comparison across validation folds

Deployment & integration

The model runs on a nightly Airflow DAG that scores every active customer account. High-risk accounts (probability > 0.65) are automatically pushed to a dedicated Salesforce queue, triggering a task for the assigned customer success manager.

Within 60 days of going live, the customer success team had intervened with 340 at-risk accounts. Of those, 78% remained active 90 days later.

Results & learnings

After six months in production the model had directly contributed to a 22% reduction in monthly voluntary churn, which translated to approximately $1.2M in annual recurring revenue retained.

The main thing I'd do differently next time: involve the customer success team much earlier in feature selection. Several of the strongest signals came from qualitative knowledge they had that wasn't captured in any database.
